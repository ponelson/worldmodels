The machines that learn how the world works.

A world model is an AI system that builds an internal picture of a physical, three-dimensional environment: it predicts how a scene evolves and how your actions change it. Where a language model predicts the next word, a world model predicts the next moment. This is a field guide to what they are, who is building them, and the hardware it takes to run them.

01  /  Definition

What a world model actually is.

A world model is an AI system that can use an internal understanding of an environment to simulate it: to predict both how the environment will evolve on its own and how an agent's actions will change it. DeepMind frames these as a stepping stone toward general intelligence, because they let an agent train inside an unlimited supply of rich, consistent simulations rather than only the real world.

Three things a language model cannot do

  • Persistence. When you look away and look back, the room is still there. Genie 3 keeps a generated world visually consistent for minutes and recalls detail you saw before, produced frame-by-frame with each new frame conditioned on the growing history of your path.
  • Physics and geometry. Water flows, light behaves, objects have volume and collide. These behaviors emerge from training on video and interaction data, not from text.
  • Consequence. The model represents how a specific action, a step forward, a turned wheel, a grasped object, changes the very next state. That closed loop is what makes it useful for robotics and planning.

Three flavors, one label

The field has no single agreed definition. Analysts sort today's systems into rough camps:

  • Renderers / generators. Produce navigable pixels or 3D scenes from a prompt. Examples: DeepMind Genie 3, World Labs Marble, Decart Oasis and Mirage.
  • Simulators. Generate physically grounded synthetic environments to train and test other systems. Example: NVIDIA Cosmos for robots and self-driving.
  • Planners / predictive models. Learn a compact internal representation and predict future states in that abstract space rather than in pixels. This is the thesis behind Yann LeCun's JEPA approach at AMI Labs.
Why now: the paradigm moved into the mainstream in late 2025 and early 2026. The bet driving the capital is that as text models commoditize, the next defensible frontier is AI that understands the physical world, and that this understanding is the missing piece for robotics and autonomy.

03  /  What they are built in

The stack, and one myth.

The honest answer to "what language are these written in" is boring: almost all of it is Python at the top, with the heavy math pushed down into GPU code. The split is simple, Python for people and CUDA/C++ for silicon, and the clever engineering lives at that boundary, keeping thousands of GPUs busy.

What you write it in

  • Python is the control and research language. It is where models are defined, trained, and evaluated.
  • PyTorch is the dominant deep-learning framework industry-wide.
  • JAX is heavily used at Google DeepMind and for large-scale research; Genie sits in Google's JAX and TPU world.
The whole point of the Python tier is expressiveness for humans. Almost none of the actual arithmetic happens here; it is dispatched downward.

What runs on the metal

  • CUDA (C and C++) is the low-level GPU programming model where the real math executes.
  • Triton, a Python-embedded kernel language, and libraries like CUTLASS are used to write performance-critical kernels.
  • OpenUSD and 3D toolchains such as NVIDIA Omniverse describe and render the scenes for simulator-style world models.
This is the tier that decides throughput. Fused kernels that avoid extra trips to memory are how these systems hit their frame-rate budgets.
Partly trueBezos is building world models.
Correction

Jeff Bezos is building an AI company, Prometheus, and is its co-CEO, but it is an "artificial general engineer" for designing physical products, which he says has nothing to do with robotics or navigable simulation. It is physical AI, adjacent to world models. Where Bezos actually touches a world-model lab, it is as an investor: Bezos Expeditions backed Yann LeCun's AMI Labs. So: builder of a neighboring thing, backer of the real thing, not a builder of world models as usually defined.

04  /  Infrastructure

The hardware underneath.

Training a world model means streaming petabytes of video into thousands of GPUs and keeping them fed. That single sentence drives every storage, memory, and rack decision below. Four interactive pieces follow: storage, the GPU/CPU division of labor, the memory hierarchy, and how the racks are built.

4a   Filesystem vs. object storage

Toggle to compare the two on the dimensions that matter for a training pipeline. Neither wins outright; real clusters use both in tiers.

Parallel filesystem

The hot tier that feeds the GPUs. Examples: Lustre, IBM Storage Scale, WEKA, VAST, DAOS, Qumulo.

The practical answer is tiered, not either-or. Object storage is the durable, cheap system of record holding the full dataset and archived checkpoints; a fast filesystem or cache is the hot tier that sustains the data rate into the GPUs. The engineering goal is to never let the expensive tier, the GPUs, wait on the cheap tier.

4b   GPU vs. CPU

Two very different processors doing two very different jobs. Toggle to see each.

GPU

    Why the split decides your bill

    GPUs are the scarce, expensive resource, so the entire system is designed to keep them near 100% utilization. The CPU's job is to feed them: load data, decode and augment video, run the training loop, and shuttle batches into GPU memory.

    If the CPU side or the storage path cannot keep up, the GPUs sit idle waiting for the next batch. That idle time, sometimes called GPU starvation, is money burned. It is exactly why the storage choice in 4a matters so much: the point of a fast hot tier is to prevent it.

    In NVIDIA's GB200 systems a Grace CPU is tied to Blackwell GPUs over a fast on-package link (NVLink-C2C), shrinking the distance between "feed" and "compute."

    4c   The memory hierarchy

    Each step down is far bigger and far slower than the one above. Click a tier. The green bar is relative speed (bandwidth and latency), roughly and on a log scale.

    The whole hierarchy exists to hide latency: keep the data the GPU needs right now in the fastest, smallest tiers, and stream the rest up just in time. For training throughput the usual binding constraints are HBM capacity (it caps how large a model or batch fits) and the storage-to-HBM path (it must sustain the data rate). Cache sizes matter, but they are a supporting act to HBM and the network.

    4d   How the racks are built

    Modern AI racks stopped being boxes of independent servers and became single, tightly-wired machines.

    Compute

    Rack-scale, not server-scale

    A GB200 NVL72 rack links 72 Blackwell GPUs and 36 Grace CPUs into one NVLink domain that behaves like a single huge accelerator, ideal for the real-time generation these models need.

    Fabric

    Two networks

    Scale-up inside the rack over NVLink (very high bandwidth, short reach). Scale-out between racks over InfiniBand or high-speed Ethernet, usually in a non-blocking, rail-optimized topology.

    Power & heat

    Liquid cooling, ~120 kW+

    A dense AI rack draws well over 100 kW, versus roughly 10–20 kW for a traditional rack. Air cannot remove that heat, so direct liquid cooling is now standard.

    Storage net

    A third network

    Separate from compute traffic, a storage fabric connects the GPU nodes to the parallel filesystem and object tier from 4a, sized to sustain the ingest rate without stalling training.

    05  /  Architecture

    Three ways to remember.

    The memory hierarchy above is not just plumbing; it decides what an AI architecture can afford to remember. Three families answer the question "how do you carry the past forward" in very different ways, and those answers land directly on the hardware. This is where a world model stops looking like a bigger language model and starts looking like a simulator.

    AttentionLLM · Transformer

    Look back at everything

    A language model predicts one token at a time. To choose the next one it attends over every prior token, and it stores those representations in a KV cache in GPU memory so it does not recompute them.

    Output
    Text tokens, code
    Remembers
    Explicit history, cached
    Cost
    Cache grows with context; longer sequences use more memory and slow each step
    The cache is the catch: it grows in step with the conversation, so it eats HBM and drags on speed as context gets long.
    RecurrenceSSM · Mamba

    Compress into one state

    A state space model also generates step by step, but it drops attention entirely. It squeezes all of history into a single fixed-size hidden state and updates it with linear state-space equations.

    ht = A·ht−1 + B·xt  →  yt = C·ht + D·xt
    Output
    Sequences: text, audio, signals
    Remembers
    Fixed-size recurrent state
    Cost
    Constant per step; flat memory no matter the length
    Because the state never grows, it can live in fast on-chip cache instead of ballooning in HBM. That is the mechanical root of "zero lag."
    SimulationWorld model

    Predict the next state

    A world model is not chasing the next word; it acts as a simulator. Given the current state and an action, it predicts the next state of an environment.

    st+1 = f( st , at )
    Output
    Pixels, 3D geometry, or latent states
    Input
    State st plus action at
    Cost
    Scales with spatial detail, not with how long you have been running
    Three output styles: video frames (Genie, Oasis), explicit 3D scenes (Marble), or abstract latents a robot can rehearse in (JEPA).

    The lines are blurring

    These camps are converging. State space models like Mamba are pulling world-model-style recurrent memory into sequence modeling, letting a language-style system carry a continuous "thought state" across very long inputs instead of re-reading an ever-growing clipboard. The prize is an architecture with the reasoning of a language model and the stateful, low-latency memory of a simulator.

    One honest caveat

    The tidy "world model equals recurrent state" picture fits the planning lineage best (Meta's JEPA, the Dreamer line), where the model rehearses in a compressed latent space. Many of 2026's headline systems, Genie, Cosmos, Oasis, are actually diffusion or transformer video models that generate frames auto-regressively, so their "memory" is carried through the generated frames and conditioning rather than a single clean state vector. Same idea, different machinery.

    Architecture note: the Transformer, state space models such as Mamba, and the JEPA and Dreamer world-model families are established, widely documented designs rather than 2026 news, so this section describes mechanics rather than fresh claims.
    06  /  The interface

    How you actually use one.

    From a user's seat, a world model feels less like a chatbot and more like stepping into a place. The loop is: describe or show, then move and change.

    In: what you give it

    • Text. A description of the place. Genie 3 generates an explorable world from a prompt alone.
    • Images and video. World Labs Marble accepts photos, video, panoramas, or a rough 3D layout and reconstructs a coherent scene.
    • A live feed. Decart's Mirage transforms streaming video in real time, so the camera itself becomes the prompt.

    Out: what you get back

    • A navigable world. You move through it in first or third person, camera under your control, rendered on the fly.
    • Persistence and editing. Revisit a spot and it is still there; remix or edit the scene; some tools export to standard 3D formats or game engines.
    • Promptable events. Ask for rain, a new object, or a different time of day and the world responds.

    Under the hood, three approaches

    1. Auto-regressive frames. Genie generates each frame conditioned on your action and the history so far, which is what gives it memory and consistency.
    2. Real-time diffusion. Decart runs diffusion fast enough for interactive video, with responses reported under 30 milliseconds for its Lucy model.
    3. Explicit 3D reconstruction. World Labs builds an actual navigable 3D scene you can move a camera through, rather than only predicting the next picture.

    The constraint that shapes everything

    1. Latency is the product. Interactivity means generating the next frame before you notice a delay. That budget, tens of milliseconds, is why the hardware in section 04 is built the way it is.
    2. Two front doors. Developers reach these models through an API; everyone else through consumer apps and demos like Project Genie or Marble.
    3. Fidelity vs. speed. Today's real-time systems trade resolution and duration (720p, a few minutes) for responsiveness. That frontier is moving fast.
    07  /  Modality

    LLM, VLM, world model.

    The same underlying idea, a large neural network predicting what comes next, becomes three different products depending on what "next" means. A language model predicts the next token. A vision-language model reads pixels and answers in tokens. A world model predicts the next state of a place you can move through. That one difference reshapes everything downstream: what you type, what you get back, and what the screen has to be.

    About a world, or a world.

    An LLM and a VLM hand you an artifact you read: an answer, a caption, a block of code. A world model hands you an environment you operate. The first two describe reality in text; the third generates a slice of it you can walk into. The cleanest way to hold the distinction: a VLM turns pixels into words, and a generative world model turns words into pixels you can move through.

Read the three as a spectrum from "answer about the world" to "operate a world," not as sealed categories.
