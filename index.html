I What a world model actually is

A model that runs the world forward in its head

A language model predicts the next token. A world model predicts the next state of an environment: the next frame, the next physical configuration, the next consequence of an action. It holds a compressed internal representation of "how this place works" and can roll that representation forward in time.

The idea is old in reinforcement learning. An agent that carries a model of its environment can plan by imagining outcomes instead of blindly trying them. The 2018 Ha and Schmidhuber paper literally titled "World Models" showed an agent training almost entirely inside its own learned dream of a racing game. What changed recently is scale and fidelity: modern world models generate photoreal, navigable, minutes-long environments you can actually walk through, or abstract representations precise enough to plan robot actions in.

The pitch

Simulate, don't just describe

Content models produce text, images, or fixed video. A world model produces an environment that reacts. That difference, reactivity, is the whole category.

Why it matters

Training grounds for agents

Robots and self-driving stacks need endless, safe, varied practice. A world model is an unlimited curriculum: generate the scenario, let the agent fail in it for free.

The fault line

Generate vs. predict

One camp renders pixels you can see. The other predicts in abstract space and never renders at all. Both call themselves world models. They are very different machines.

The distinction that trips people up: a video generator (Sora, Veo) makes a clip and stops. A world model keeps a persistent internal state and answers the question "if I turn left, what do I see?" in real time. A learned dynamics model is the real core: it captures how the world evolves and how actions change it. Real-time interactive rendering is the most visible form of that, not the universal test. A system can be a world model and never render a pixel, as the JEPA family below shows.
II Anatomy of the machine

What is actually inside a world model

Before the market and the metal, the machine itself. Almost every world model, however different on the surface, is built from the same chain of parts. Pick an archetype to see which parts it leans on, what it stores, what it predicts, and what it emits.

The rollout problem: the dynamics part predicts the next state, then feeds its own prediction back in to predict the one after that. Small errors compound. The further the rollout runs, the more the imagined future drifts from a plausible one. Holding a world coherent over a long horizon is the central open problem, and it is why "looks good for ten seconds" and "is a usable world model" are different claims.

VII Languages & frameworks

What they are actually written in

The honest answer: overwhelmingly Python at the top, C++/CUDA at the bottom, and a thin band of specialized languages in between. The bars are an editorial prevalence estimate, not survey data, and they are not shares (they need not sum to 100). They rank roughly how common each language is in real world-model work today.

On Julia, since you asked: Julia was created at MIT by Jeff Bezanson, Stefan Karpinski, Viral B. Shah, and Alan Edelman. It was first announced publicly in 2012 and reached its stable 1.0 release in 2018. Its pitch is "the speed of C with the ergonomics of Python" for numerical and scientific computing. In world models it lives almost entirely in the scientific-ML / differentiable-simulation niche (the SciML ecosystem, DifferentialEquations.jl, physics-informed models), which overlaps the physics-accuracy goals of world models. But it is not a mainstream choice for the large generative or JEPA-style world models, which are PyTorch- and JAX-first. If someone claims a frontier world model is "built in Julia," that is a flag to verify, not to repeat.
VIII The division of compute

GPU does the math, CPU keeps it fed

A world model is trained and served on GPUs, but a GPU that runs dry is money on fire. The CPU's job is to make sure the accelerators never wait. In video-scale world models this feeding problem is often the real bottleneck, not raw FLOPs.

CPU

latency · branching · orchestration

A handful of powerful, general cores. Good at unpredictable, sequential, decision-heavy work. In an NVIDIA Grace-Blackwell node the Grace CPU sits right next to the GPUs to shovel data at them.

  • Reading and decoding the training corpus off storage
  • Data-loader pipeline: shuffle, batch, augment, tokenize video
  • Orchestration: schedulers, checkpting logic, control flow
  • Serving the interaction loop: input handling, session state
  • Anything with branches, I/O waits, or one-thing-at-a-time logic

GPU

throughput · parallel · tensor math

Tens of thousands of small cores plus dedicated tensor and video engines. Built to do the same operation across enormous arrays at once, which is exactly what a neural network is.

  • Every forward and backward pass through the model
  • Diffusion / autoregressive frame generation at inference
  • Attention over long spatiotemporal context (the expensive part)
  • Hardware video decode/encode (NVDEC/NVENC) for frame I/O
  • The real-time render loop for interactive worlds
The trap: teams buy GPUs and then starve them because the data pipeline (CPU + storage + network) cannot deliver frames fast enough. World models make this worse than LLMs, because the training unit is high-resolution video, not text. Feeding the GPU is a storage and interconnect problem as much as a compute one, which is why the next three sections matter.
IX The memory tiers

Seven tiers, from nanoseconds to milliseconds, kilobytes to petabytes

Every level trades speed for capacity. World models strain this hierarchy harder than almost any workload because their working set, long video context plus activations, is enormous. Tap a tier to inspect it.

Where world models bind: the binding constraint is almost always HBM capacity and bandwidth. A long-horizon video context and its attention state have to live in GPU memory, and there is never enough. That pressure pushes state down the tiers: activations and KV/context spill to NVMe, and datasets and checkpoints live on the network file and object tiers. How fast you can pull them back up is the whole ballgame.

X Filesystem vs. object storage

Why a filesystem earns its keep next to S3

You asked the real question: if blob storage is cheap and infinite, why put a filesystem in front of it? Because training and interactive serving demand access patterns that object storage was never designed for. Here is the honest side-by-side.

The working answer: tier, don't choose

Most large stacks avoid leaning on either extreme: object storage alone struggles to feed GPUs at full tilt, and all-flash for a whole petabyte-scale video corpus is rarely worth the cost. The usual answer is to tier by temperature:

Hot working set (the shards actively feeding GPUs, checkpoints being written, the interaction session state) lives on a high-performance filesystem backed by NVMe, where random reads, memory-mapping, partial writes, strong consistency, and high metadata rates all exist.

Cold and archival data (the raw multi-petabyte video corpus, old checkpoints) lives on object storage, cheap and durable, pulled up and cached on the file tier only when a training run needs it. The durable win is this cost asymmetry: the all-flash tier is where speed lives, the object tier is where scale lives, and a caching filesystem is what lets one feed the other without paying flash prices for cold bytes.

Four legitimate patterns, not one. Real stacks pick among: (1) a shared parallel filesystem; (2) object storage with a local NVMe cache; (3) object-native sharded streaming with range reads; (4) a tiered or hybrid namespace that presents object storage as files. Which one wins depends on the workload shape, not on any single vendor thesis.
Checkpoints are the sleeper cost: a large model checkpoints huge state, frequently, across every node at once. That write storm is bursty and massively parallel. Systems that overwrite files in place lean hard on a parallel filesystem; even those that write fresh sharded objects still need enormous aggregate write bandwidth. Either way, checkpoint throughput often justifies the fast tier.

Adjacent, not inside

Storage lives in its own racks

All-flash NVMe file/NAS nodes for the hot tier and denser nodes for the object tier sit in separate racks, connected to the compute fabric. Keeping storage off the compute rack lets each scale on its own curve: you add GPUs for FLOPs and add flash nodes for feed bandwidth independently.

Two bottlenecks, two fixes: if the model does not fit or trains too slowly, you add compute racks. If the GPUs sit idle waiting for data, you add storage and network bandwidth. Conflating the two is how expensive clusters end up half-utilized.
XII The interface

How you actually talk to a world model

Interaction has two distinct modes. First you create a world from a prompt. Then, for the interactive families, you enter a tight real-time loop where your actions become the model's next input. The loop is the hard part.

Creation: many doors in

You seed a world with whatever describes it: a text prompt, a single image, a video clip, a rough spatial sketch or blockout (World Labs' Chisel works this way), or a panorama. Physical-AI stacks also seed from real sensor and camera logs so the generated world matches a specific robot or vehicle.

Interaction: the real-time control loop

For interactive worlds this loop runs many times a second. At 24 frames per second you have roughly 40 milliseconds per cycle to read input, generate the next frame consistently with everything before it, and display it. Miss the budget and the world stutters or drifts.

Not every family renders: a JEPA-style planner has no visual interface at all. Its "interface" is a stream of observations in and predicted future representations out, consumed by a planner or robot controller, never by human eyes. There is no frame to look at, which is exactly the point: it spends no compute drawing pixels it does not need.
